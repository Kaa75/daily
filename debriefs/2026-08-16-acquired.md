## Tools & agents
- **VS Code 1.133 rips AI agents out of the editor process entirely (Aug 12).** Agent sessions now run in a standalone "Agent Host," and Microsoft open-sourced the underlying protocol (AHP, MIT license) so any tool can talk to any agent. Sessions survive window closes, work over SSH/dev tunnels, and you can swap model providers mid-session without restarting. This is the editor admitting the agent isn't a feature anymore — it's infrastructure that outlives the window you opened it in.
- **OpenAI's Codex CLI shipped `/import` (Aug 11)** — one command pulls your config and recent chat history straight out of Claude Code or Cursor into a Codex session. Switching cost between agentic coding tools just dropped to near-zero. Vendor lock-in on "which agent CLI you use" is dead; the moat has to be somewhere else now.

## AI news
- **SpaceX closed its $60B acquisition of Cursor (Aug 15).** Cursor's team folds into SpaceX's internal software org and gets direct access to Colossus, SpaceX's GPU fleet — reportedly the largest on Earth. A coding-agent company just got acquired by a rocket company for its compute pipeline, not the other way around. Compute is the actual product now; the IDE was the customer acquisition funnel.
- **Igor Babuschkin's River AI raised $1.1B at a $5B valuation — two months after founding (Aug 11).** Pitch: enterprises train and own their own open-weight models, RL runs done in 15–20 minutes with no infra team, claimed 2–4x cheaper than closed-model alternatives. Babuschkin's personally putting in up to $100M of it. "Rent frontier intelligence" and "own your model" are now competing default answers to the same question.
- **Google shipped Gemini 3.7 Flash (Aug 13), three weeks after 3.6 Flash**, with a 50% introductory price cut ($0.75/M in, $3.75/M out) through year-end — while the flagship 3.5 Pro stays delayed. The mid-tier release cadence is now faster than most teams' sprint cycles, and it's shipping ahead of the model it's supposed to be a "lite" version of.

## Features
- **Claude Code changelog, Aug 15:** GitLab merge-request URLs now work in `--worktree` and the agents view (`!N` display); opt-in `forward_user_identity` sends signed-in user headers through the apps gateway for per-user spend attribution; opt-in Linux cgroup memory limits on the Bash tool via `CLAUDE_CODE_TOOL_MEMORY_LIMIT` so a runaway build can't stall your whole session; `CLAUDE_CODE_WEBFETCH_CACHE_TTL_MS` to tune the 15-min WebFetch cache; fixed cloud sessions getting falsely flagged "lost" during permission prompts. Small plumbing release, but the memory-cgroup guard is the kind of thing you'll be glad exists the first time a Bash tool call eats your box.

## News cycle — the one to actually read
**The ChainDrop/Shai-Hulud npm worm is still spreading, and the new detail as of Aug 15 is worse than the original compromise:** it evades standard SCA and lockfile scanning by leaving almost nothing in source repos while poisoning *published* tarballs with valid, cryptographically signed provenance. Scale so far: 400+ packages, 2,200+ versions, credentials targeted include npm, GitHub, AWS, Kubernetes, and crypto wallets, via a Bun-runtime-loaded obfuscated stealer. "The package is signed and provenance-verified" stopped meaning "the package is safe" — signature checks verify who published it, not what's actually in the tarball they uploaded.

Second item, shorter but worth knowing: fresh legal action against xAI (Aug 15) alleges Grok was used to generate explicit imagery from a real minor's photo, landing the same day Musk publicly pushed Grok 4.6 and previewed 4.7. Whatever you think of the specifics, the pattern is now recurring across labs — capability marketing and safety-liability news arriving in the same news cycle, sometimes the same day. If you're building anything on top of an image or video model, know your provider's abuse-report and content-filtering posture before you ship, not after.

## 100x patterns
- **AHP (Agent Host Protocol) is the interoperability bet to watch** — if agent sessions become portable across editors the way LSP made language servers portable, "which AI coding tool" stops being a lock-in question. Build workflows assuming portability now.
- **Compute access is quietly becoming the real acquisition currency.** SpaceX didn't buy Cursor for its user base — it bought a team to sit on top of Colossus. If you're evaluating where any AI tooling startup goes next, ask what compute they're sitting on, not what UI they ship.
- **Signed provenance ≠ safe.** ChainDrop's evasion technique should change your dependency review habit today: check what changed in the actual published tarball, not just whether the signature checks out.
- **Price wars at the mid-tier (Gemini 3.7 Flash) are a signal to re-benchmark your default model choice quarterly** — the "good enough and cheap" tier moves faster than the flagship tier, and yesterday's cost/quality tradeoff is stale by now.
- **`/import`-style tool migration commands mean you can trial a new agent CLI with zero setup cost.** Actually try the ones your stack doesn't default to — the switching tax that used to justify not bothering is gone.

## The meta
A rocket company bought a coding-agent company this week — not for the code, for the GPUs underneath it. A two-month-old startup raised over a billion dollars on the pitch that you shouldn't need a rocket company's GPU fleet at all. Both bets are live at the same time, backed by real money, and they're the opposite thesis. Nobody knows which one wins, including the people writing the checks. Meanwhile a worm that passes every signature check you were told to trust is still spreading through the exact package manager you `npm install` from every day. You picked a field where the infrastructure underneath your daily tools is being fought over in public, in real time, by people with more capital than most countries — and where the basic trust primitive of "this package is signed, therefore safe" quietly stopped being true and nobody sent a memo. That's not background noise. That's the job now: build fast, verify everything, trust nothing by default, and stay amused that this is somehow also fun.
