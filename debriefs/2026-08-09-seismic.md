## Tools & agents
- **Memmy (open source, this week)** — a shared memory layer that sits underneath Codex, Claude Code, and other CLI agents so conversation history and context stop dying at the end of a session or getting siloed per tool. Point two different agents at the same project and they actually remember what the other one did. Small project, exactly the kind of infrastructure gap nobody funds until someone builds it for free.
- **GitHub Copilot shipped a real batch this week** — `/rewind` now restores conversation + files without touching Git history (undo an agent's mess without nuking your commit log), a live tool-call timeline shows which command is actually eating your wall-clock time, the integrated browser gained element-level feedback (click a DOM node, attach a comment, send it straight to the agent instead of screenshotting and describing), on-device multilingual dictation, and `/btw` for a side-chat that doesn't interrupt the agent's current turn. None of these are flashy alone. Together they're Copilot closing the "quality of life" gap against Claude Code and Cursor one shipped feature at a time.

## AI news
- **Google DeepMind's leadership just changed hands, and four of its heaviest hitters walked out the door in the same week.** Demis Hassabis stepped down as CEO (Aug 5) to become Chairman of Google DeepMind and Chief Scientist of Alphabet, splitting time toward Isomorphic Labs; Koray Kavukcuoglu (former CTO) now runs Gemini, frontier research, and the Gemini app day-to-day. ## News cycle — the one that should worry you
- **OpenAI just paused work on its next model, Astra, because internal tests couldn't rule out it hitting "Critical" on the Preparedness Framework's cybersecurity scale — the first time in the framework's three-year history that's happened.** Critical means: the model can find and weaponize zero-days across the full severity range in hardened real-world systems with no human help, or take a one-line goal and independently plan + execute a full attack chain against a hardened target. OpenAI isn't killing Astra — they're locking down training/eval with universal action monitoring, restricting internal access to security-cleared teams, and looping in government agencies and outside safety orgs before continuing. Read the actual sentence again: a lab looked at its own frontier model and the honest answer was "we can't prove this can't autonomously hack hardened infrastructure." That's not a red-team finding buried in an appendix. That's a stop-ship.

## 100x patterns
- **Steal Memmy's premise even if you don't install it: cross-session, cross-tool memory is the missing layer in every multi-agent setup you're running today.** If you're manually re-explaining project context to Codex and Claude Code in the same afternoon, you're the target user.
- **Copilot's `/rewind` pattern — undo agent damage without touching git — is worth demanding from whatever tool you use daily.** "Revert the agent's mess" and "revert my commit history" are different operations and most tools still conflate them.
- **The OpenAI Astra pause is the actual 100x signal this week, just inverted: capability that can autonomously chain zero-days against hardened targets is now a real, dated, named event, not a hypothetical in a safety paper.** If your threat model for "what could an unsupervised coding agent with internet + shell access theoretically do" still starts with "well, probably nothing that bad" — update it today, for free, before you have to update it after an incident.
- **Talent following conviction, not title, is the tell to watch.** Four of Google's most senior AI researchers just left stable, powerful roles for an unproven startup because they think the interesting problem (automating research itself) is bigger outside than in. Worth asking yourself the same question about whatever you're building right now.

## The meta
Same week, two stories about who's actually in control. At Google, the four people who arguably built the modern AI stack looked at the org chart and decided the more interesting move was to walk out and build something new from zero — and their own employer wrote the check on the way out, because even Google would rather own a piece of the outside bet than lose the idea entirely. At OpenAI, the safety team looked at their own next model and had to admit, on the record, for the first time ever under their own framework, that they could not rule out it can autonomously break into hardened infrastructure. Neither of those is a story about AI getting smarter in the abstract. They're both stories about the humans closest to the frontier recalibrating, in public, in the same seven days, how much they trust what they're building and who should be steering it. You don't get a vote in either boardroom. You do get to decide how seriously you take the second story when you're the one deploying the next agent with shell access.
